## Why ASC Ownership Changes the Entire Equation

A physician who operates at a hospital and at a surgeon-owned ASC lives in two different economic worlds. In the hospital, the surgeon collects a professional fee (the physician's billing). In the ASC, the surgeon collects a professional fee **plus a proportionate share of the facility fee** — the income generated by the operating room itself.

Capital equipment in a surgeon-owned ASC does three things simultaneously that no other investment structure replicates:

1. **Generates an immediate personal tax deduction** — flows through the entity to the surgeon's individual return in the year of purchase
2. **Unlocks procedure revenue from day one** — the equipment enables case categories that produce facility fee distributions
3. **Increases the equity value of the ownership stake** — additional EBITDA, multiplied by the ASC valuation multiple, accrues to the surgeon at sale or recapitalization

The interplay of all three makes the equipment-ownership conversation fundamentally different from any standard capital purchase.

## The Tax Mechanics: How the Deduction Flows to the Surgeon's Return

### Entity Structure Primer

Surgeon-owned ASCs are predominantly structured as:
- **Multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership** (most common — maximum flexibility, partnership-level income/loss allocation, no one-class-of-stock restriction)
- **S-corporation** (less common for multi-physician ASCs due to shareholder limitations but used for tighter governance structures)

In both cases, the entity files an informational return (Form 1065 for partnerships, Form 1120-S for S-corps) and issues each surgeon-owner a **Schedule K-1** reporting their pro-rata share of income, deductions, and credits. The ASC itself pays no income tax. Everything flows to the individual.

### Step-by-Step Flow for a $200,000 Equipment Purchase

**Assume:** 4-surgeon ASC LLC, each owning 25%. ASC purchases Arvis for $200,000 and elects 100% bonus depreciation.

1. ASC files Form 1065 for the tax year; reports $200,000 bonus depreciation deduction
2. Each surgeon's K-1 reflects **$50,000** (25% × $200,000) as an ordinary business deduction
3. Each surgeon reports the K-1 on their personal Form 1040, Schedule E, Part II
4. Each surgeon claims $50,000 on Form 4562 — reducing personal taxable income
5. At 37% marginal rate: each surgeon saves **$18,500** in personal federal income tax

Total entity-level deduction: $200,000. Total tax savings across the 4-surgeon group: **$74,000**. In the year of purchase.

### Partnership vs. S-Corp: The Income Limitation Distinction

For **partnerships and LLCs**: the Section 179 income limitation is tested at the **individual partner level**. A surgeon's W-2 income, self-employment income from their practice, and other active business income all count toward their individual capacity to absorb the deduction. A surgeon earning $800,000 from their surgical practice absorbs a $50,000 K-1 deduction without any income limitation concern.

For **S-corporations**: Section 179 passed to shareholders is limited to each shareholder's pro-rata share of the corporation's aggregate active business income. If the S-corp itself has a $200,000 loss year (rare for a going-concern ASC, but possible in a startup year), the Section 179 deduction may be constrained at the entity level.

**Bonus depreciation advantage:** Unlike Section 179, bonus depreciation has **no income limitation at any level**. A pass-through loss generated by bonus depreciation flows to each surgeon-owner's K-1 and can be used against other income streams — subject to the at-risk (Section 465) and passive activity (Section 469) rules. Surgeons who materially participate in the ASC (which most active physician-owners do) generally satisfy these requirements.

| Method | Year 1 Tax Savings | NPV of All-Year Savings (8%) | Lost Value vs. Bonus Dep. |

## How Distributions Work: The Cash Flow to Surgeon-Partners

### The Legal Framework

ASC profit distributions must be made **strictly pro-rata to ownership percentage** — not based on case volume, procedure count, or any other usage metric. This is required by the Stark Law/Anti-Kickback Statute safe harbor framework. Volume-based distribution structures violate safe harbor requirements and create federal fraud and abuse exposure.

### The Quarterly Distribution Cycle

Most surgeon-owned ASCs distribute profits quarterly:

1. **Reserve setting:** Management committee sets aside 2–4 months of operating reserves and capital replacement reserves
2. **Tax distributions (if included in operating agreement):** Mandatory quarterly distributions calculated to cover each surgeon-owner's estimated income tax liability on their allocated share — typically sized at 40–45% effective rate to cover federal plus state. This prevents surgeons from owing taxes on income they have not yet received in cash
3. **Pro-rata distributions:** Remaining profits distributed to owners in proportion to equity stake

**Illustrative distribution for a center generating $2M annual EBITDA, 4-surgeon equal ownership:**
- Each surgeon's 25% share: **$500,000/year in pre-tax distributions**
- Less personal income tax (~37%): ~$185,000
- After-tax distribution: **~$315,000/year per surgeon-partner**

### The Tax Treatment of Distributions

For LLC/partnership ASCs: surgeons are taxed on their **allocated share of income** (from K-1) regardless of whether cash is actually distributed. Cash distributions reduce the surgeon's basis in their ownership interest and are generally non-taxable up to that basis. Distributions exceeding basis trigger capital gain recognition — which only occurs when cumulative distributions exceed the surgeon's total invested capital and allocated income to date.

## Equity Value: The Multiplier That Changes the Math

### EBITDA Multiples for Orthopedic ASCs (2025–2026)

| ASC Type | EBITDA Multiple Range |
|---|---|
| Single-specialty physician-owned | 5x–8x |
| **Orthopedic-focused ASC** | **7x–10x** |
| Multi-specialty physician-owned | 6x–10x |
| Regional/national platform (USPI, SCA, Surgery Partners) | 11x–17x |
| Non-controlling physician minority interests | 3x–6x |

*USPI (United Surgical Partners) reported ~38% EBITDA margins in recent periods. Physician-owned orthopedic ASCs without management company overhead typically reach 25–35% EBITDA margins at scale.*

### The Equity Multiplication Model

Every dollar of EBITDA added to an orthopedic ASC through owned equipment capability is multiplied at the valuation multiple. This is the math that separates "equipment purchase" from "capital investment":

**Scenario: $200,000 Arvis generates $225,000 in incremental EBITDA/year**

| Multiple Applied | Enterprise Value Added | 25% Owner's Equity Gain | Net of $31,500 After-Tax Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7x | $1,575,000 | **$393,750** | **+$362,250** |
| 9x | $2,025,000 | **$506,250** | **+$474,750** |
| 10x | $2,250,000 | **$562,500** | **+$531,000** |

The return on a $31,500 net after-tax capital contribution (25% share of $200K after tax) — measured by equity value creation alone, before distributions — is **12x–18x** at 7–10x EBITDA multiples. This assumes the incremental cases are durable and reflected in trailing EBITDA at the time of a recapitalization or sale.

### How Equipment Affects Valuation in Practice

Two mechanisms drive the value creation:

**Mechanism 1: Eliminated rental expense → direct EBITDA improvement**
An ASC currently renting C-arm fluoroscopy at $20,000/year eliminates that line item upon purchase. The $20,000/year flows directly into EBITDA. At 9x: **$180,000 in enterprise value** created by removing a $20,000 annual expense. The equipment pays for itself before the first case is performed.

**Mechanism 2: New procedure capability → revenue EBITDA**
Navigation-enabled cases that could not previously be performed (revision arthroplasty, complex spine) add entirely new revenue streams at contribution margins of 40–65%. This EBITDA is valued at the full multiple at exit.

## Self-Employment Tax: The S-Corp Consideration

For surgeon-owners who are also active operators of the ASC, the structure of their ownership interest affects SE tax exposure:

- **Partnership/LLC (general partner or active member):** Distributive share is generally subject to self-employment tax at the 2.9% Medicare rate (plus 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax above $200K/$250K) for active participants. Most surgeons are already above the $176,100 Social Security wage base through W-2 compensation from their practice — so SE tax on ASC distributions is primarily the 2.9–3.8% Medicare component
- **S-corporation shareholders:** Distributions from the S-corp are **not subject to payroll taxes** — only the surgeon's W-2 salary from the S-corp is. This structure eliminates SE tax on the distribution portion

For a surgeon receiving $500,000 in ASC distributions through a partnership structure (vs. an S-corp), the SE tax differential on distributions is approximately **$14,500–$19,000/year** at 2.9–3.8%. Over a decade of ownership, that is $145,000–$190,000 — material enough to justify the operational complexity of an S-corp election in some situations.
